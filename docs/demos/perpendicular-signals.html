Perpendicular signals between two lines

A signal always leaves its sender at exactly 90° to the sender. What it hits the receiver at is 90° only when the two lines are parallel.

1 — Both lines vertical

Parallel: leaves A at 90°, arrives at B at 90°. Nothing to see, which is the point.

2 — The tilt passed back and forth

Line A starts tilted by the tilt angle. Its signals meet B at 90° − tilt. B takes the tilt on and A returns to its original position, so on the next round B is the tilted one sending back. The tilt is handed over, not accumulated — what leaves the sender is gone from the sender — and the mismatch stays the same size each round, just leaning the other way.

Both sides participate in every subsegment. Neither is held: at each nested oscillation A and B both tilt, each taking part of the angle, and they converge instead of one handing over to the other.

The same two poles, in space. Two directions always lie in a plane, so the tilting is still planar — the third dimension only lets that plane sit anywhere. Turn the view to see it edge-on, and the flat diagram is what you get.

Three poles make a triangle of edges: every pole exchanges with every other, all at once, and the tilt splits three ways instead of two. The pole count is a slider, so the split is tilt/N rather than a hard-coded half.
